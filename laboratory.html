WHY THE BEAMS SEPARATE

A little more mass.
A wider arc.

In a magnetic sector, the magnetic force supplies the centripetal force. For positive ions moving perpendicular to the field, every isotope bends in the same direction. The field changes the direction of motion, not the ion’s speed.

Here all five titanium isotopes carry one positive charge and are accelerated through the same voltage. They have the same kinetic energy, but different speeds. The heavier isotope follows a larger radius and arrives at a different collector position. Faraday cups collect the separated ion currents, which amplifiers turn into measurable voltages.

The difference is small: r₅₀ / r₄₆ ≈ √(50 / 46) ≈ 1.043—only about 4.3% in radius. The main arcs preserve that difference; only the collector inset spreads the exit positions out vertically.

FOLLOW THE PHYSICS

Magnetic force = centripetal force

qvB = mv² / r

Energy gained through a voltage V

½mv² = qV

Combine them to find the radius

r = mv / (qB)
= √(2mV / q) / B

m = ion mass · q = positive charge · v = speed
V = accelerating voltage · B = magnetic field strength

What this simplified diagram shows

This is an ideal, uniform 180° magnetic sector with negligible initial energy spread and no fringe fields. The main circular paths share an entry point and tangent; their radii use √m, approximating isotope masses by their mass numbers. Outside the field, the beams travel in straight lines into the cup openings. The inset multiplies vertical collector spacing by 12; cup sizes and the overall length scale are illustrative.

A real LA-MC-ICP-MS adds ion optics, an electrostatic analyzer, focusing and instrument-specific sector geometry. This drawing explains magnetic mass dispersion, rather than reproducing the layout or collector spacing of a particular instrument.

What dissolves?
What stays behind?

At the Indian Institute of Science, I carried out acid-leaching experiments on silicate rocks spanning ultramafic to felsic compositions. I used non-HF acids and High-Pressure Asher digestion to investigate cation mobility during weathering and sample dissolution.

Comparing the chemistry of leachates and residues helped investigate mineral stability and the behavior of major and rare-earth elements. I also worked on bulk geochemical characterization of the Sadiya meteorite.

PREVIOUS RESEARCH · ARIZONA STATE UNIVERSITY

Read the isotope record
within a grain.

I worked on developing and refining protocols for high-precision, in-situ titanium isotope measurements of calcium–aluminum-rich inclusions (CAIs), including FUN CAIs, using laser-ablation multicollector inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry: LA-MC-ICP-MS.

Laser ablation samples a small area of a solid. The resulting aerosol enters the plasma, and a multicollector mass spectrometer measures isotope signals. This preserves spatial context within a heterogeneous sample.

ASU RESEARCH · FURNACE WORK AT UNIVERSITY OF ARIZONA

Build the reference.
Improve the measurement.

I synthesized and documented titanium-doped calcium–magnesium–aluminum–silicate (CMAS) glasses, using a gas-mixing furnace at the University of Arizona in Tucson.

Glasses with varied vanadium and chromium ratios served as internal standards for the titanium isotope analytical work. This linked materials preparation directly to the development of measurement protocols.

PHYSICAL RESEARCH LABORATORY · SUMMER 2022

Amoeboid olivine aggregates

At PRL, Ahmedabad, I investigated amoeboid olivine aggregates in the Mukundpura and Allende chondrites using a JEOL 8530F field-emission electron probe, including EDS and WDS analyses.

Comparing mineral chemistry and textures helped investigate nebular processing and the different alteration histories preserved by the two meteorites.
